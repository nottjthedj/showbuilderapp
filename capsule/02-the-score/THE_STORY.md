# "The Score" — the whole story

The complete narrative of the film, written to be **heard**: one narrator, start to
finish, roughly ten minutes read at a measured pace. This is the story, not the run
sheet — for how the night is actually operated, see [`NOTE.md`](NOTE.md) and the
`film[]` beats in `gtad.show.json`.

---

Every city tells a story about itself.

Ours is simple. Four gangs. One city. And a vault in the middle of it with eight figures inside.

All four of them think they're the main character.

The old family thinks it's about respect. The cartel thinks it's about power. The kings on the block think it's about loyalty. And the suits downtown think it's a spreadsheet.

They're all wrong. But we'll get to that.

---

Start with the Corvettis.

Don Salvatore Corvetti — Sally Cufflinks — is somewhere past seventy-five, and he has never once raised his voice, because he has never had to. Gold pocket square. Red carnation. A back room behind a restaurant where the light is always warm.

He has a son named Vinnie who sweats through every shirt he owns and asks questions the Don finds exhausting. When the Don says move the product, Vinnie asks what the product is. The Don eats a cannoli, thinks about it for a long moment, and says: respect. And also the drop.

Then there's Los Halcóns.

Rico Delgado — El Halcón — is what happens when confidence stops asking permission. White linen suit open to the middle of his chest, a gold falcon on a chain, a scar over one eyebrow, and a full-grown Bengal tiger named Sugar who lies on his white couch and does not care about any of this. Rico believes power is how loud you can be. Nobody has ever told him otherwise, because in Rico's house nobody else gets a turn.

Across town, the Street Kings.

Marcus Wells — Big Deal — is the calmest man in this whole story. Fresh fade, one gold chain, a chrome-and-cyan lowrider sitting on hydraulics. Marcus has never needed to prove anything to anybody. Riding with him is Lil' Problem, who is always eating, always talking, and is currently building — out loud, at length, in detail — a taco empire funded entirely by money nobody has stolen yet.

And in a glass tower lit violet: The Firm.

Preston Ashford Sterling the Third has reinvented theft. No masks. No guns. Just a proprietary infiltration solution. He calls it Breach. It is in private beta.

His analyst Kayleigh has watched the entire founder demo and has notes — chiefly that when Preston asks who in this city is leaking, the answer is Preston, because Preston posts everything. He takes this as engagement.

---

Two more voices in this city.

One is Detective Dutch McGraw. Twenty years, one trench coat, a toothpick, and a wall of faces. He is looking for a man with no face and a vault with no bottom. Every single year, he almost has him.

The other is President Maximillian Diamante, who will tell you this city is safer than it has ever been. Believe him. He knows all the criminals personally. Great people. Total winners.

---

And then there's the one nobody can name.

Ask any of the four crews who really runs this city and they go quiet. Then they tell you about a man in a black three-piece suit who has never been photographed. Black gloves. Always turned away. He's in every story and in none of them.

The Corvettis are certain he takes a cut. Rico is certain he has been inside the house. Preston has a file on him with nothing in it.

He is the reason all four of them are careful. He is the reason McGraw has spent twenty years chasing smoke.

Remember him. He matters far more than he exists.

---

There's one more person in this, and he's the only one who talks directly to you.

TJ. He runs the room. On the mic he's the Handler — the voice that cuts across all four crews like a pirate signal across four radio stations. When the story needs telling, he tells it. When the games run, he steps to the decks and runs them.

And on the first night he says the only thing you actually need to know: four gangs are about to go to war over that vault, every one of them is going to need soldiers, and that's you.

Don't get wasted. Don't get busted. And when this city starts lying to you — and it will — the only voice in this room you can trust is coming out of that booth.

---

It starts in a police lineup.

McGraw has five people up against a height marker. One Corvetti. One Halcón. One Street King. One Firm suit. And one civilian who wandered in off the dance floor and is holding his booking number upside down.

McGraw knows one of them knows the man with no face. He points at the wrong one. He always points at the wrong one.

Out on the block, the Street Kings clock the trouble in the mirror — one cop, then four, then a helicopter — while Lil' Problem is still describing the taco fleet.

In a mirrored pink mansion, somebody sends targets to Rico Delgado's house. A tracer round goes past his cheek and he does not blink. He isn't frightened. He's insulted.

And in a warm gold back room, the Don hands Vinnie a package he is not allowed to look at, open, or discuss.

---

Then the job gets real.

Preston runs a loyalty audit on the entire city and eventually locates the leak in a mirror. He demos Breach, it shrieks in his hands, the room flips emergency red, and he calls that a soft launch.

The Corvettis and the Halcóns shake hands on a fifty-fifty split. Neither man lets go of the other's hand. And behind them, completely unnoticed, a tiger picks up a bag of money and walks out of the building with it.

There's a blueprint that has to be memorized in ten seconds. Preston has taken two courses in this. Rico doesn't need the paper — Rico remembers every face that ever crossed him. Rico also cannot remember the first icon.

It's always the falcon.

There's a night on the block where the convoy comes together. There's a wall at four in the morning, a searchlight on a timer, and a sidekick who has eaten the nachos of a lookout who never existed.

And there's a rain-soaked pier where the old man and the young one square off under five starting lights. Rico says: this is my city, old man.

And the Don looks up — at a single lit window high above the water, where one gloved hand rests flat against the glass — and says: it's nobody's city, boy. We just rent it.

From him.

---

Everything lands at a gala.

The President is at a podium under a banner reading WAR ON CRIME, telling a four-colour ballroom that this city stands united against crime — while four envelopes, one in each colour, slide into his jacket at the same time. He has never, ever taken a dollar from any of these fine… patrons. Four more years.

All four crews file in behind him. McGraw is at the buffet with a shrimp halfway to his mouth and absolutely no idea.

In the middle of the room is the vault. One four-colour code opens it.

A champagne glass goes off the edge of the podium in slow motion. And when it hits the floor, every phone in that building is trying to crack the same combination.

---

Here's how it ends.

The boats are in the Keys. The cash is loaded. The cops lost everybody at the bridge. And the vault door finally groans open — onto an empty room.

Not emptied. Empty. Bare concrete, one hard overhead light, and nothing else at all. There were never eight figures in there. There was never anything in there.

Four gangs tore a city apart over a room with nothing in it.

The Don wants to know where the money is. So does Rico. So does Marcus. Preston refreshes an app and reports that the money is… pending.

On the floor, under the light, there's a single folded note with two letters written on it. "— H."

Nobody ever finds out who that was. That's the joke. There was nobody to find. The man with no face was a story four crews told each other until all four believed it, and they spent an entire night at war with a rumour.

And then McGraw kicks the door in, gun up, twenty years of certainty behind him — and arrests the President by mistake.

---

Which leaves the only part of tonight that was ever true.

The money was never the score. The score was the room.

Two thousand people who walked in as strangers, picked a colour, took a side, played every game, and spent a whole night turning into a crew. Four gangs fought over that vault all night and walked out with nothing.

You danced. You played. You got made. And you're walking out with a night nobody can ever take off you.

That's the only score in this city that ever pays out.

There's always another one. Same crew. Next night. Don't be late.

Welcome to After-Dark. You're made now.

---

One last thing.

McGraw, alone in the empty vault, finds a phone on the floor. Still logged in. Still playing. Under a handle that doesn't match any of the four gangs.

He turns it over. He looks up.

And for the first time in twenty years, he's right.
